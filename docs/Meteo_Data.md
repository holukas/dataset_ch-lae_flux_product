## Meteo products (notebooks `01`-`08`)

Eight notebooks turn the screened tower measurements into the finished meteo products, one file per variable. Each takes its variable from the database to a corrected, checked series on disk, with enough provenance that a downstream user can tell a measurement from an estimate.

All eight are built much the same way. Each pulls the quality-screened half-hourly measurements from the database, where the older `mst` screening covers the years up to 2021 and the newer `diive` screening takes over from 2022, and joins the two with `combine_first`. Timestamps go to local time and to the middle of the half-hour bin. Then the August 2012 logger clock error gets its `+15.5 h` shift, and the periods ruined by the tower power failure and the autumn storm are deleted, for every variable that sat on that logger. Checks run, and a parquet plus a CSV lands in the external data folder. Where the eight differ is in how far each goes toward a complete series, and in how much independent evidence stands behind its decisions. Outside sensors do that work: MeteoSwiss Lägern 2.5 km away, NABEL at 49 m on the same tower, which stops in 2018, and for rain the MeteoSwiss gauge at Ehrendingen 3.8 km away, since Lägern measures no precipitation at all — plus, for `PREC` alone, the thirteen MeteoSwiss stations within 21 km, which answer a question no single reference can. Depending on the variable they act as fill sources or as diagnostic references, and each one passes an alignment guard first, so the 15-minute timestamp conversion is proven before anything trusts it.

Notebook [`01_METEO_SW_IN`](notebooks/10_METEO/30_PRODUCTS/01_METEO_SW_IN_2004-2025.html) sets the pattern the others follow. It builds the gap-free record of incoming shortwave radiation for 2004-2025 out of the tower sensor at 47 m, helped by two others: the NABEL pyranometer at 49 m on the same tower, which runs until 2018, and MeteoSwiss Lägern, which covers the whole period. On top of the shared repairs it removes the small false signal a pyranometer reports at night, subtracted day by day so that nighttime reads exactly zero. That correction is handled carefully, because forcing every nighttime record to zero will quietly turn a missing one into an apparent measurement. Whatever gaps remain go to an XGBoost model that predicts the tower reading from the calculated position of the sun and from the helper sensors. It runs twice, once up to 2018 and once after, since the later years have only Lägern to lean on and their fills are the less certain ones. What comes out is complete, never negative, zero at night, and carries a flag marking every half hour as measured or filled and by which method.

[`TA`](notebooks/10_METEO/30_PRODUCTS/02_METEO_TA_2004-2025.html) and [`PPFD_IN`](notebooks/10_METEO/30_PRODUCTS/03_METEO_PPFD_IN_2004-2025.html) stay closest to it. Air temperature is filled by a general XGBoost model with three drivers: NABEL temperature where it exists, Lägern temperature, and the gap-filled `SW_IN` from notebook 01, since sunshine is what drives the daily temperature swing. It runs twice, once over the NABEL years and once over the rest, because a fill backed by a sensor on the same tower deserves a different label than one backed by a station 2.5 km off. `PPFD_IN` has no twin of its own. It borrows the gap-filled `SW_IN` and Lägern global radiation instead, which works because photon flux and shortwave radiation are nearly the same quantity in different units, and since all its drivers span the whole record there is no period split. Like `SW_IN` it gets the nighttime zero-offset correction, and its nighttime gaps are set to zero by physics instead of being modelled. Both export a complete series with an `ISFILLED` flag naming the method behind every value. Gaps shorter than an hour are interpolated.

[`RH`](notebooks/10_METEO/30_PRODUCTS/04_METEO_RH_2004-2025.html) is not modelled at all. Every gap is filled from another measurement of the same quantity: NABEL at 49 m first, then Lägern for whatever NABEL cannot reach, each corrected by a month-by-month offset fitted on the overlap, because the two sensors drift apart seasonally and also across the years. The notebook strips the drift offset out of records reading above 100%, and its flag says where a value came from, not which model made it. [`PA`](notebooks/10_METEO/30_PRODUCTS/05_METEO_PA_2004-2025.html) and [`LW_IN`](notebooks/10_METEO/30_PRODUCTS/06_METEO_LW_IN_2005-2025.html) ship as measured, gaps included. Both records are already about 99% complete, and a filled pressure value would look exactly like a real one to anyone downstream with no flag to check. Pressure's real work is a unit break at 2021/2022: one source is stored in kPa, the other in Pa. The notebook decides which from the magnitude of the data, cross-checks that verdict against the database's own unit tag, and raises if the two disagree. Longwave has no second sensor anywhere, not even at MeteoSwiss, so its 2012 corrections stand on provenance instead of measurement. That gets stated outright rather than dressed up as a passed test.

[`VPD`](notebooks/10_METEO/30_PRODUCTS/07_METEO_VPD_2004-2025.html) downloads nothing and models nothing. It computes vapour pressure deficit from the finished `TA` and `RH` files by formula. Both inputs are complete, so every record gets a value, and the flag says what it was computed from: two real measurements, a gap-filled temperature, or an `RH` reconstructed from NABEL or from the weaker Lägern transfer.

[`PREC`](notebooks/10_METEO/30_PRODUCTS/08_METEO_PREC_2004-2025.html) is the odd one out. One rain gauge measured throughout, but in mid-2018 it moved from EMPA's acquisition system to the ETH logger, and against the MeteoSwiss gauge at Ehrendingen the amounts it records jump by roughly a factor of 1.3. The notebook locates that break blind, scanning for the month where the catch ratio steps hardest, and only then quotes the fieldbook entries that name it. A source flag says which acquisition era each value belongs to, including 71 transition days that could not be settled either way.

Which side of that break is wrong took a second reference to answer. One gauge can show that the tower changed; it cannot show whether the tower or the gauge was the thing that moved. So the notebook brings in every MeteoSwiss precipitation station within 21 km, thirteen of them, and the answer is not close: the early era reads low against every single one, while the later era agrees with the network. The stations also give a precipitation-elevation gradient, and measured against it the early era is a quarter short of what a gauge at 690 m should catch while the later era is a tenth short, which is the ordinary wind under-catch of an exposed ridge. The early record is the broken one, and the 2018 rewiring repaired it. A second column ships that correction, the early era scaled onto the later era's level month by month, and the measured column is left exactly as measured. The distinction matters: the correction makes the two halves comparable, it does not make either of them true, and both keep the tower's own under-catch.

Gaps are filled from Ehrendingen where that is possible, and the method was picked by hiding thousands of stretches of real data and scoring nine candidates against what was hidden. A monthly rescaling factor won. It is good for totals and not for the timing of any single half hour, which the notebook states in those terms, and the fill turns out to be least reliable on the shortest gaps rather than the longest. Before September 2014 the reference is daily only, so those gaps stay empty instead of being invented. The 2012 repairs are skipped here, since the gauge was still on EMPA's system then and off the tower logger, and the notebook tests for the clock shift and rejects it rather than assuming.

All eight check themselves the same way. Guards are handed deliberately bad input to prove they raise, written files are read back and compared against what was in memory, and each export tests completeness, physical range, and a continuous 30-minute index with no duplicates.

## Timeshift checks

### Summary
I checked for potential time shifts (wrong timestamps) by comparing the maximum of measured short-wave incoming radiation (SW_IN​) with the maximum potential radiation on clear-sky days (filtered for clear days, 70% of potential, to remove cloudy noise). The results show a small time lag in the `SW_IN_T1_47_1_gfXGB` variable starting in 2022, the same year the `diive` meteoscreening notebooks were deployed. To isolate the source of this offset, I performed numerous diagnostic tests on the processing steps. The extensive testing did not reveal any processing error or bug; all output values were correct.
### Details
**No time shift due to resampling.** I tested the resampling output in the `diive` meteoscreening notebook, which downloads data from the database. I resampled the original 1min data that is stored in the database to 30min for the raw data variable (with name `SW_IN_T1_47_1` in the database). At timestamp `2022-08-01 09:30:00`, the value was `654.801507`. This time range corresponds to the values between `2022-08-01 09:01:00` and `2022-08-01 09:30:00` in the original 1min raw data files. I calculated the average from the original 1min raw data files manually and got `654.8015067`. This means there is no timeshift due to resampling.

**Reproduce database value at timestamp.** I then ran the meteoscreening notebook again, but this time I also included the nighttime-offset correction (corrects for values below zero, which also changes values during daytime). The value was `665.219439`, corrected for an approx. -10 offset because of sub-zero nighttime values. The same value was also confirmed for quality-screened and nighttime-offset-corrected data downloaded from the database. 

**No error during merging.** After all different data sources for `SW_IN` were merged, the value was still the same (no error due to merging). After the merging of all `SW_IN` variables, the nighttime offset correction is run again, this time on all merged data instead of the individual data sources. After this second offset correction, the value changed to `664.929319`. Since the value became slightly lower, this means that the offset was positive (above zero during nighttime) and the respective positive offset value was subtracted. All data were then stored to a parquet file.

**No error due to middle timestamp.** During gap-filling of `SW_IN_T1_47_1` data are loaded from the parquet file. During this loading, the timestamp is converted to `TIMESTAMP_MIDDLE`. The value before gap-filling and after loading all data from the file at the corresponding middle timestamp `2022-08-01 09:15:00` (corresponding to the half-hour between 9:00 and 9:30) was `664.929319`, so up to here everything is correct. I originally assumed that an error could occur here because of the 15min shift of the middle timestamp compared to the typical timestamp that shows the end of the averaging period. 

**No error due to gap-filling.** After gap-filling `SW_IN`, the value was still the same (there was no gap in this location anyway so the value is not expected to change). After the gap-filling of other meteo variables and subsequent merging with flux data the value also remained the same (notebook 22.3). As an additional check, I manually calculated the **air temperature** value at `9:15` directly from original 1min raw data files between `09:01` and `09:30`, and the check was OK, values were identical.

**Identical results for FFT analysis.** Next I ran the FFT analysis directly using the original data file for Aug 2022, using the 1min raw data file. Then I downloaded data for Aug 2022 from the database and also ran the analysis. The results from both analyses were identical, meaning that the data in the database and in the original raw data files are the same. 

**Same results for file data and database data.** I then resampled the 1min data downloaded from the database to 30min, using the same functions as are used during the meteoscreening in `diive`, and I also created the middle timestamp for the resampled data. I then checked the value of `SW_IN_T1_47_1` at timestamp `2022-08-01 09:15`, it was `654.801507`, the same value as for the very first test described above (*No time shift due to resampling*). Running the FFT analysis on 1min raw data files or 1min data from the database yielded the same results, with a time shift median of 6.39MIN, similar to what can be seen in the figure below (@fig-phaseshiftfft-16-25) in 2022. All steps so far produced the correct results.

**Shift can be seen in original raw data files.** I checked if this offset in 2022 can also be seen in the 1min data instead of the 30min data when downloaded from the database. Above checks already confirmed that data in the database are the same as in the original raw data files. This test also showed an offset of 5.90MIN, similar to the previous test for 30MIN data. This means this shift can be confirmed for the original raw data. I also tested the FFT analysis for data Jul 2021, 1min raw data files vs 1min data from database. Both runs yielded the same result. Also the same result was achieved for 1min raw data files resampled to 30min vs 1min database data resampled to 30min.

I was not able to find any error in any of the processing steps and scripts.
